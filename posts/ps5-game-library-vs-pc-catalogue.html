Libraries

The PS5 game library vs the PC catalogue

One library is curated around a handful of large games. The other is a catalogue of everything ever made. That difference shapes almost everything about how you spend money on each platform.

Published 15 December 2025 · Updated 15 December 2025 · 8 min read · by PC Games vs PlayStation

People tend to compare the two libraries by asking which has more games in it. That is the wrong question, and the answer is not close. The useful question is how each library is built, because that determines what you can buy, when you can buy it, and how much you end up paying.

The two libraries have different shapes

The PlayStation library is a curated set. A comparatively small number of very large, heavily promoted releases arrive on the console each year, and they dominate the conversation because that is how the platform is marketed and funded. Underneath them is a long tail of smaller titles.

The PC catalogue is cumulative. Every game released on PC over decades is available, and the newest releases are one category among thousands. It is not curated in any meaningful sense; it is simply accumulated.

This produces a strange asymmetry that is worth sitting with. On PlayStation you have a short list of things to choose from this year. On PC you have no list, only a search box.

Exclusives are the whole argument

Exclusives exist for a reason. A studio that sells millions of copies on one platform gets money to make the sequel, and players get games that would otherwise not exist. A console without exclusives has no reason to exist.

So exclusives are not a marketing trick that should be dismissed. They are the mechanism by which the platform funds content.

What has changed is the arithmetic. Exclusivity windows used to be measured in years. Now they are usually measured in weeks or a few months for most multiplatform titles, and a large number of games have no console exclusivity window at all. The long tail of permanently console-only games is much shorter than it was.

The honest summary: for a large and growing share of games, "will it come to PC" is now a question with a predictable answer, and "will it stay on PlayStation forever" is a much rarer situation. If you own one platform and play mostly multiplatform games, you are unlikely to feel locked out for long.

Buying the same game twice

This is the most practically important difference, and the one most often glossed over.

A digital PlayStation purchase and a PC purchase are separate purchases at separate prices. There is no general mechanism for carrying a game you bought on one platform to the other. If you buy a game on PS5 and it arrives on PC two months later, you buy it again at whatever the PC price is.

If you own only one platform, this never affects you. If you own both, or if you and a household member own different platforms, it becomes a real and recurring cost.

Discounts and backlog

Here the PC catalogue wins clearly, and by a large margin.

  • Price cuts. PC games are routinely reduced, sometimes heavily, and the deepest discounts accumulate as a game ages. Console pricing is higher and cuts less often.
  • Bundle keys. PC games are frequently bundled with graphics cards and other hardware, which is a genuine and repeatable way to acquire games cheaply if you are buying components anyway.
  • Older titles. Years of PC catalogue are available at low prices. The equivalent console back catalogue is thinner and less consistently discounted.
  • Free to keep. PC games are very often given away free during sales events.

On console, two things partially offset this. Physical disc editions are resellable, so a purchase is not entirely sunk. And subscriptions, covered next, spread cost rather than reducing it.

Subscriptions on both platforms

Both platforms now have a subscription, and they are aimed at different things.

The PlayStation tiers focus on giving you access to a rotating catalogue of games, with the top tier adding online multiplayer and a monthly game to keep. The value of the top tier is largely the online access and the day-one trial, since the catalogue rotation means you cannot keep what you play.

PC subscriptions work differently. The core offer is a rotating catalogue, with an additional tier that lets you play games from the catalogue during a limited activation window rather than only while subscribed. That distinction matters a lot if you play a smaller number of games carefully.

Neither is a substitute for buying games you care about. Both are worth calculating against your actual habits before you subscribe.

Backwards compatibility

The PS5 plays a large part of the PS4 library, and older PlayStation titles become available on newer hardware over time. If you owned a PS4, a PS5 arrives with a library attached, which meaningfully changes the value calculation for anyone who bought one.

PC has no equivalent need for this, because the whole catalogue is already there. What PC has instead is that some older games have stopped working on modern operating systems, or depend on services that have been shut down. This is rare but real, and it is worth checking before buying an old title on PC.

A few things only PC has

  • Mods. Community modifications that change graphics, add content or alter gameplay.
  • Community tools. Frame rate counters, performance overlays and similar.
  • Controller freedom. Play any game with any input device. On console you are largely limited to what the console supports.
  • Ultra-wide. Wider fields of view in games that support it.

Why each platform attracts different players

Look at what each library contains and the audience follows.

The PlayStation library is heavily weighted towards large, story-driven, single-player experiences, and that reflects who buys the console. Those games are often best with a controller on a television, and that hardware choice reinforces the software one.

The PC catalogue contains the full range: the same story-driven games, plus competitive multiplayer, simulations, strategy titles, mods and older software. That variety pulls in a wider and more technical audience, including people who came to PC gaming for work and stayed for games.

This is why "which platform is better" is such a fruitless question. The two libraries are not competing for the same player as often as the marketing implies.

Which library should you choose?

  • Choose the console if you want first access to the large exclusive games, if you prefer owning a physical copy you can resell, or if buying one game at full price on one platform suits you better than managing a wide catalogue.
  • Choose PC if you play a broad mix of genres, if you enjoy picking up older and discounted titles, if you value the total cost of owning a large library, or if you want to be able to try anything.
  • Owning both is the strongest option for cost per game played, and the weakest for the money you have available up front.
