PS5 vs PC: which one should you buy?

The console and the PC are both good at gaming. They are good at different things. This guide is about working out which set of things is yours.

Published 12 January 2026 · Updated 12 January 2026 · 9 min read · by PC Games vs PlayStation

Most buying guides on this subject start by declaring a winner. We are not going to, because there isn't one. A console and a PC occupy different positions in your life, and the right comparison is not which machine is faster. It is which machine fits how you actually play, and what you will spend on everything around it.

Start with the games, not the hardware

Ask yourself one question: is there a specific game that only exists on PlayStation, or that arrives on PC so much later that you would play the console version now?

If yes, buy the console. That single fact outweighs every performance argument on this page, and it is not close. Console exclusives are the entire reason the platform exists.

If no, you should genuinely consider PC, because you are comparing a fixed, finished appliance against a machine you will keep improving. Write down the three or four games you actually play. If they are all on both platforms, your decision is about cost and capability rather than access.

The cost comparison people get wrong

Comparing the console price against the sum of PC parts is the most common mistake, and it cuts both ways.

On the console side, forget what is not included

A console box contains a console and one controller. Everything else is extra:

  • A second controller if more than one person plays
  • A headset if you play with other people in the room
  • Extra storage, because the built-in drive fills up quickly once you install a few large games
  • A new television, if yours cannot do 120 Hz

None of these are unusual costs. They are just costs that the sticker price does not show.

On the PC side, count the whole build

A PC price is almost always a parts price. On top of it you usually need:

  • A monitor. A good gaming display is not cheap, and it is the single largest hidden cost in a PC purchase.
  • A keyboard and mouse, or a controller if you prefer one.
  • Possibly an operating system licence, depending on the parts you buy.
  • A desk, chair and decent lighting, if the machine will be your main workstation.

Once both sides are counted honestly, the gap narrows considerably. It rarely disappears.

Settings: the real technical difference

A console is a fixed target. Developers know exactly what hardware every PS5 has, so they tune to it. That has a real benefit: a console version usually holds a steady frame rate once it has been patched.

A PC has a much wider range of hardware, which is why a port can look noticeably sharper or run at a higher frame rate. On PC you also get:

  • Higher refresh rates, if your display supports them
  • VRR, which smooths the experience when a frame rate dips slightly
  • Upscaling options that let a mid-range card look better than its raw specs suggest
  • Ultrawide support for the games that offer it
  • A settings menu you control rather than one the developer picks

If you care about image quality and you have a decent television, this is the strongest technical argument for PC. Our guide on why PC ports often look better goes into the detail.

The upgrade path is where PC wins outright

This is the argument that convinces most people, and it is not subtle.

Console upgrades mean buying a new console. A four-year-old machine is simply a four-year-old machine, and the games it cannot run are gone to you. Some games can be expanded with official storage, and that genuinely extends its life, but the processor and graphics are fixed.

A PC is different. In four years you can replace the graphics card and memory, or the storage, or all three, and keep the case, the processor and everything else that is still adequate. Your machine's useful life is not capped by when you bought it. For anyone who keeps a console a long time, this is worth more than any launch title.

Competitive play is not a close call

If you play shooters competitively, or intend to, this section decides the question for you.

Mouse and keyboard give you a level of aim precision and movement speed that no controller matches. Combined with a high refresh rate display and low input latency, that combination is the standard for competitive play for a reason. Console versions of competitive shooters frequently restrict your settings too, so you are often playing on lower settings because the platform requires it, not because you chose it.

We have covered the input question in detail in DualSense versus mouse and keyboard.

Living room use favours the console

Put the two side by side in a real living room. The console is small, silent, starts in seconds, and is controlled entirely from the sofa. The PC needs a monitor that makes desktop windows legible, and either a keyboard you are not using or a controller for everything. If you want to plug in and sit down, that difference is not trivial.

There is no wrong answer here. Some people will hate being asked to use a keyboard on a sofa. Others will never use the console's interface once they get used to a PC.

Our recommendation, as a set of rules

  1. Any exclusive you care about is available — buy PlayStation. This is the deciding factor.
  2. No exclusives, competitive player — buy PC. Nothing else competes with mouse, keyboard and high refresh rate.
  3. No exclusives, casual, shared television — buy PlayStation. Less friction, fewer things to get wrong.
  4. No exclusives, you already use PCs daily — buy PC. You will get more out of it and you can maintain it.
  5. No exclusives, none of the above — it is genuinely close. Compare the total cost including peripherals, and pick whichever you will not resent owning in four years.

Before buying PC parts, read our step-by-step buying guide. The most expensive mistake in PC gaming is mismatched components.

What we deliberately did not do here

You will notice there are no frame-rate numbers on this page. That is intentional. A single figure without naming the game, the settings, the television and the exact parts would be misleading, and comparing a console's 4K mode against a PC's settings menu is not a meaningful measurement.

We also do not take a position on which machine is "better". A question that has no answer should not be given one for the sake of a headline.
